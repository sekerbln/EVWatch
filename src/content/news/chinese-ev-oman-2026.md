Walk into any major mall in Muscat today and you're likely to find a Chinese EV showroom nearby. What was a curiosity two years ago has quietly become a mainstream retail presence. But the real story isn't the brands already here — it's the second wave now preparing to enter.

## Who Is Already in Oman

**BYD** is the most established Chinese EV presence in the country. The brand now distributes five models through three Omani showrooms, operated by World Auto Business, which recently inaugurated a flagship facility that BYD described as "setting a new benchmark in automotive retail." Available models span from the entry-level Dolphin to the Seal sedan and Atto 3 SUV.

**MG Motor** — technically a Chinese-owned British brand under SAIC — has taken a different approach, using aggressive financing deals to challenge Japanese incumbents in the volume segment. MG's strategy is less about EV evangelism and more about replacing the Corolla and Camry buyer with an affordable, well-specced alternative.

Together, these two brands have done the groundwork of normalizing Chinese automotive products in a market historically dominated by Toyota, Nissan, and Hyundai.

## The Market They're Entering

Oman's EV market is still small in absolute terms, but the structural conditions are shifting fast. Private individuals account for nearly three quarters of EV ownership, meaning retail buyer trust — not fleet procurement — is what moves the market here. Government and public-sector fleets are expected to grow significantly through 2030, which could accelerate adoption further if Oman follows Saudi Arabia's lead in EV fleet mandates.

Battery capacity tells an interesting story: smaller battery EVs (under 50 kWh) currently dominate, suggesting buyers are prioritizing affordability and urban commuting range over long-distance capability. This actually favors Chinese brands, which have historically excelled in the sub-$40,000 segment.

The broader trajectory is clear. Chinese automotive brands are expected to capture 34% of the Middle East and Africa vehicle market by 2030, up from roughly 10% in 2024. Oman, sitting at the intersection of GCC infrastructure investment and a young, price-conscious population, is squarely in the path of that shift.

## The Second Wave: Who's Coming Next

The brands to watch aren't the ones already here — they're the ones preparing their GCC entry strategies right now.

**Zeekr** (Geely's premium EV sub-brand) has built its reputation on fast charging technology. The Zeekr 001 demonstrated 546 kW peak charging in 2024, and its 7X SUV is positioned against European luxury brands on performance metrics. Zeekr's partnership with Shell for charging infrastructure deployment gives it a credible path to market in regions where public charging is still developing.

**Li Auto** has already confirmed its GCC ambitions. The brand's Extended Range Electric Vehicle (EREV) technology — a small petrol generator that eliminates battery range anxiety — is arguably the most GCC-appropriate EV architecture available today. For a country like Oman where inter-city driving on desert highways is common and fast charging corridors are still sparse, an EREV removes the single biggest objection most buyers have to going electric.

**Xpeng** is pursuing aggressive international expansion, having entered Southeast Asia and Europe in 2025. Its AI-first product philosophy — including a vision-based autonomous driving system that doesn't require LiDAR — gives it a genuine technology story beyond price competition. The G9 SUV has been specifically cited by analysts as suited to tech-forward markets in the Gulf.

![NIO display](/assets/news/nio-es9.jpeg)
**NIO** remains the most complex case. Its Battery-as-a-Service model and proprietary swap stations are compelling technology, but require significant infrastructure investment in each market. Without swap stations, the NIO value proposition loses its core differentiator. A GCC entry would require either a local infrastructure partner or a shift to conventional fast charging as the primary offering.

## The Charging Question

Oman's charging infrastructure is the market's most significant constraint. While Dubai now has over 1,270 public CCS2-compatible charging points, Muscat lags considerably. The Chinese brands best positioned for Oman are therefore those with either EREV technology (Li Auto), battery swap capability (NIO), or those that can operate within existing infrastructure (BYD, which uses the widely adopted CCS2 standard).

New entrants will need to be honest with buyers about real-world charging options rather than leading with peak charging statistics that assume infrastructure that doesn't yet exist outside the capital.

## What This Means for Buyers

The next 18 months will likely see more Chinese brand announcements for the Omani market than the previous three years combined. Domestic saturation in China is pushing every major manufacturer to find overseas volume, and the Gulf — with no tariffs on Chinese vehicles, growing government EV mandates, and consumers increasingly open to Chinese brands — is a logical priority market.

For buyers, this creates a real opportunity: more competition, better pricing, and more product choice. But it also requires more due diligence. Not every brand that announces a GCC presence will build the after-sales network to back it up. The questions to ask any new entrant remain the same: How many authorized service centers? What are the warranty terms for GCC-spec vehicles? And who do I call when something goes wrong?

BYD has had years to work through these questions in Oman. The next wave of brands will need to answer them much faster.
